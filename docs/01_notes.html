1. SDOF Systems: Theory & Simulation

Motivation (5 minutes)

Every vibration analysis—whether we are predicting the resonance of an aircraft wing, isolating a sensitive optical table, or sizing a vehicle suspension—begins with a single, critical step: deriving the governing Equation of Motion (EOM).

Modern computational tools can integrate an ODE in milliseconds, but they cannot tell you whether your physical model is right. If you miss a geometric coupling term, mis-sign a restoring force, or drop an inertial contribution, the solver will give you a highly precise wrong answer. Today, we focus on D’Alembert’s Principle, a formulation method that turns complex dynamics into intuitive, statics-style equilibrium problems.

From Newton’s Second Law to Dynamic Equilibrium (15 Minutes)

For a rigid body of mass \(m\) and mass moment of inertia \(I_G\) about its center of mass \(G\), Newtonian mechanics tells us that net external forces and moments produce linear and angular accelerations:

\[ \sum \mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}} = m\mathbf{a}_G \] \[ \sum \mathbf{M}_{G,\text{ext}} = I_G\boldsymbol{\alpha} \]

In 1743, Jean le Rond d’Alembert proposed a deceptively simple algebraic rearrangement: move the right-hand side to the left.

\[ \sum \mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}} + \underbrace{(-m\mathbf{a}_G)}_{\mathbf{F}_I} = \mathbf{0} \]

\[ \sum \mathbf{M}_{G,\text{ext}} + \underbrace{(-I_G\boldsymbol{\alpha})}_{\mathbf{M}_I} = \mathbf{0} \]

Here, we define two fictitious—yet mathematically exact—quantities: 1. Inertial Force: \(\mathbf{F}_I = -m\mathbf{a}_G\), acting at the center of mass \(G\) in the direction opposite to the assumed positive acceleration \(\mathbf{a}_G\). 2. Inertial Couple: \(\mathbf{M}_I = -I_G\boldsymbol{\alpha}\), acting in the direction opposite to the assumed positive angular acceleration \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\).

With these two terms added to our Free-Body Diagram (FBD), the moving system is now in dynamic equilibrium. Every tool you mastered in Statics—summing forces, taking moments about any convenient point, and eliminating unknown reaction forces—applies directly.

Example

Consider a uniform rigid bar of mass \(m\) and length \(L\), pinned at its left end \(O\) (where \(I_O = \frac{1}{3}mL^2\)). At static equilibrium, the bar rests horizontally, supported by a linear spring of stiffness \(k\) attached at distance \(a\) from \(O\), and a viscous damper of coefficient \(c\) attached at distance \(b\) from \(O\). A vertical harmonic force \(F(t) = F_0 \cos(\omega t)\) is applied downward at the right tip (\(x = L\)).

Step 1: Static Equilibrium Check

Before disturbing the bar, its weight \(mg\) (acting at \(L/2\)) compresses the spring by a static deflection \(\delta_{\text{st}}\). Taking static moments about \(O\):\[mg\left(\frac{L}{2}\right) - (k\delta_{\text{st}})a = 0 \implies k\delta_{\text{st}}a = mg\frac{L}{2}\] When we measure \(\theta\) from this horizontal static equilibrium position, the constant gravity moment and the static spring preload cancel out identically (for small angles). Always verify this explicitly before dropping gravity!

Step 2: Kinematics

Assume a positive clockwise rotation \(\theta\) (with angular velocity \(\dot{\theta}\) and angular acceleration \(\ddot{\theta}\)):

  1. Spring vertical displacement: \(y_a = a\sin\theta\)
  2. Damper vertical velocity: \(\dot{y}_b = \frac{d}{dt}(b\sin\theta) = b\dot{\theta}\cos\theta\)
  3. Horizontal moment arms (perpendicular distances from \(O\) to vertical forces): \(a\cos\theta\), \(b\cos\theta\), and \(L\cos\theta\).

Step 3: D’Alembert Dynamic Equilibrium about Pivot \(O\)

On our dynamic FBD, we draw: 1. The spring restoring force \(F_s = k y_a\) pushing upward (opposing clockwise rotation) 2. The damper force \(F_d = c \dot{y}_b\) pushing upward (opposing clockwise velocity). 3. The external tip force \(F(t)\) pushing downward (aiding clockwise rotation). 4. The inertial couple \(M_I = I_O\ddot{\theta}\) acting counterclockwise (opposing positive clockwise \(\ddot{\theta}\)). Summing moments about \(O\) (taking clockwise as positive):

\[ \sum M_O = -I_O\ddot{\theta} - (c\dot{y}_b)(b\cos\theta) - (k y_a)(a\cos\theta) + F(t)(L\cos\theta) = 0 \]

Step 4: Exact Nonlinear Equation of Motion

Substituting \(y_a = a\sin\theta\) and \(\dot{y}_b = b\dot{\theta}\cos\theta\), and moving the inertial, damping, and stiffness terms to the left-hand side:

\[ I_O\ddot{\theta} + c b^2 \cos^2(\theta)\dot{\theta} + k a^2 \sin(\theta)\cos(\theta) = F_0 L \cos(\theta)\cos(\omega t) \]

Notice that even though our spring and damper are linear components, the geometry of rotation introduces nonlinear terms (\(\cos^2\theta\), \(\sin\theta\cos\theta\)).

Step 5: Small-Angle Linearization & Canonical SDOF Form

For small oscillations about equilibrium (\(\theta \ll 1\text{ rad}\)), we apply \(\sin\theta \approx \theta\) and \(\cos\theta \approx 1\):

\[ \underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{3}mL^2\right)}_{I_{\text{eq}}}\ddot{\theta} + \underbrace{\left(cb^2\right)}_{c_{\text{eq}}}\dot{\theta} + \underbrace{\left(ka^2\right)}_{k_{\text{eq}}}\theta = F_0 L \cos(\omega t) \]

Step 6: Physical Intuition & Design Takeaways

By reducing the assembly to canonical SDOF form (\(I_{\text{eq}}\ddot{\theta} + c_{\text{eq}}\dot{\theta} + k_{\text{eq}}\theta = M_{\text{eq}}(t)\)), we can read off the system’s physics immediately:

  1. Undamped Natural Frequency:\[\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{k_{\text{eq}}}{I_{\text{eq}}}} = \sqrt{\frac{3ka^2}{mL^2}} = \frac{a}{L}\sqrt{\frac{3k}{m}}\]
  2. Design Lever Arm: Moving the spring closer to the tip (\(a \to L\)) increases the effective stiffness quadratically (\(k_{\text{eq}} \propto a^2\)) and scales the natural frequency linearly with \(a\). Similarly, moving the damper outward increases effective damping as \(b^2\).

In-Class Concept Check & Bridge to Lab (10 Minutes)

  1. Sign-Check Intuition: What would happen to our static equilibrium cancellation if the bar were oriented vertically (like an inverted pendulum) instead of horizontally? (Answer: Gravity no longer has a constant horizontal lever arm; it introduces a destabilizing \(-mg\frac{L}{2}\sin\theta\) term that directly reduces \(k_{\text{eq}}\).)
  2. Limits of Linearization: At what initial angle \(\theta_0\) do you think our small-angle assumption (\(\sin\theta \approx \theta, \cos\theta \approx 1\)) starts to noticeably fail—\(5^\circ\)? \(20^\circ\)? \(45^\circ\)?
